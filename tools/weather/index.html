Heat & Temperature

Everything in weather starts with heat. The sun bathes the earth in radiation, but it doesn't heat the air directly — sunlight passes through the atmosphere mostly unabsorbed. Instead, it heats the ground. The ground, now warm, radiates infrared energy back upward, and that's what heats the air from below.

This is why the hottest part of the day isn't noon — when the sun is strongest — but a couple of hours later, after the ground has had time to absorb energy and re-emit it into the air above. It's also why pavement shimmers on a summer afternoon: you're seeing the heated air rising off the surface.

As you move upward through the atmosphere, the air gets cooler. This decrease is called the lapse rate, and in the lower atmosphere (the troposphere, where all weather happens) it averages about 6.5 °C per kilometer. The lapse rate isn't just a fun fact — it's the foundation of atmospheric instability, and therefore of nearly all weather phenomena.

Temperature decreases with altitude at the lapse rate. Adjust the surface temperature and lapse rate to see how the profile changes.

Why does temperature drop with altitude? Because air pressure decreases as you go up (there's less air above you pushing down). As air rises into lower-pressure regions, it expands, and expansion cools a gas. This is adiabatic cooling — no heat is added or removed; the temperature changes purely because the volume changes.

The sun heats the ground; the ground heats the air from below. Temperature decreases with altitude at the lapse rate. This vertical temperature structure is what drives all weather — it determines whether air stays put or starts moving.

With this foundation — solar heating, the lapse rate, and adiabatic processes — we can start to understand why air moves. And once air moves, we have weather.

Pressure & Density

Air has weight. The entire column of atmosphere above you, stretching up to the edge of space, presses down with a force of about 10 tonnes per square meter. That's atmospheric pressure. At sea level it averages 1013 millibars (or hectopascals — the same number, different name).

Pressure decreases with altitude, roughly halving every 5.5 kilometers. This isn't linear — it follows an exponential curve, because the air at each level must support the weight of all the air above it, and there's progressively less air to support.

Here's the critical link to temperature: warm air is less dense than cold air. When you heat a parcel of air, its molecules move faster and spread apart, lowering its density. This lighter air is buoyant — it rises, like a hot-air balloon. Cold air, being denser, sinks.

Warm particles (red) are lighter and rise; cool particles (blue) are denser and sink. Adjust the heat to see how temperature drives vertical motion.

This buoyancy mechanism is the engine that converts thermal energy into kinetic energy — from heat to motion. When the sun heats the ground unevenly (dark asphalt absorbs more than a grassy field; land heats faster than water), it creates regions of warm, rising air and cool, sinking air. These pressure differences are the direct cause of wind.

ρ = P / (R × T)

The ideal gas law relates pressure, density, and temperature. At constant pressure, raising the temperature lowers the density. At constant altitude, a warm column of air exerts less surface pressure than a cold one, because its lower density means there's less mass in the column.

Warm air is less dense and rises; cold air is denser and sinks. This simple relationship between temperature, density, and buoyancy is the fundamental engine of all atmospheric motion.

Wind

Wind is air moving from high pressure toward low pressure. That's it. Every breeze, gale, and hurricane is ultimately driven by a pressure gradient — a difference in pressure between two locations.

Think of it like water on a tilted surface. The steeper the tilt, the faster the water flows. In the atmosphere, the "tilt" is the pressure gradient: the greater the difference in pressure over a given distance, the stronger the wind. Meteorologists draw isobars — lines of equal pressure on a map — and the wind blows roughly perpendicular to them, from the high side to the low side.

Wind vectors flow from the high-pressure region (H) to the low-pressure region (L). Increase the difference to strengthen the wind.

On a local scale, you can feel this every day. The sea breeze that picks up on a summer afternoon happens because land heats faster than water. The air over the land warms, rises, and creates a low-pressure zone. The cooler, higher-pressure air over the ocean rushes in to fill the gap — that's your sea breeze. At night, the pattern reverses.

F = −∇P / ρ

The pressure gradient force (the negative gradient of pressure divided by density) is the force that accelerates air from high pressure to low. If this were the only force acting, wind would blow straight across isobars. But the earth is rotating, and that introduces a complication that changes everything about how wind patterns look on a large scale.

Wind is driven by pressure gradients. Air accelerates from high-pressure regions toward low-pressure regions. The stronger the gradient, the stronger the wind.

The Coriolis Effect

If the earth didn't rotate, wind would blow in straight lines from high pressure to low. But the earth does rotate, and this rotation deflects moving air in a way that is subtle, counter-intuitive, and profoundly important.

The deflection is called the Coriolis effect. In the northern hemisphere, moving air is deflected to the right of its direction of travel. In the southern hemisphere, it's deflected to the left. The effect is zero at the equator and strongest at the poles.

Why does this happen? Because the earth's surface moves at different speeds at different latitudes. At the equator, a point on the surface is racing eastward at about 1,670 km/h. At 60° latitude, that speed is only half as much. When air moves from the equator toward the poles, it carries its eastward momentum with it — but the ground beneath it is moving slower, so the air appears to curve eastward (to the right in the northern hemisphere).

Left: straight path in a non-rotating frame. Right: the same motion seen from the rotating earth's surface. The Coriolis deflection curves the path to the right in the northern hemisphere.

The Coriolis effect doesn't create wind — pressure gradients do that. But it shapes how wind flows around pressure systems. Instead of rushing straight into a low-pressure center, air spirals around it. In the northern hemisphere, air flows counter-clockwise around lows (cyclones) and clockwise around highs (anticyclones). The southern hemisphere reverses both.

At large scales, the Coriolis effect also creates a balance with the pressure gradient force. When these two forces are equal, wind flows parallel to the isobars instead of across them. This is called geostrophic balance, and it explains why upper-level winds tend to follow the contours of pressure systems rather than cutting across them.

Earth's rotation deflects moving air — to the right in the northern hemisphere, to the left in the southern. This creates the spiral wind patterns around pressure systems and is essential for understanding large-scale weather.

Convection

When the ground heats air from below, that air becomes buoyant and rises. As it rises, it cools (adiabatic cooling). Eventually it reaches a height where it's no longer warmer than the surrounding air, and it stops rising. It then spreads horizontally, cools further, and sinks back down. This circular flow is a convection cell.

You see convection everywhere. The shimmer above a hot road. The rolling boil in a pot of water. Cumulus clouds forming in neat rows on a summer afternoon — each cloud marks the top of a convection cell where rising air has cooled enough for moisture to condense.

The strength of convection depends on how quickly temperature decreases with altitude — the environmental lapse rate. If the actual lapse rate is steeper than the adiabatic lapse rate (about 10 °C/km for dry air), then a rising parcel stays warmer than its surroundings and keeps accelerating upward. This is an unstable atmosphere, and it produces vigorous convection.

Warm air rises in the center of each cell, cools and spreads at the top, then sinks at the edges. Color indicates temperature.

If the lapse rate is shallower (the atmosphere cools slowly with height), a rising parcel quickly becomes colder than its surroundings and sinks back. This is a stable atmosphere — it resists vertical motion and tends to produce calm, clear conditions.

unstable: environmental lapse rate > adiabatic lapse rate

Convection is the mechanism that transports heat upward from the surface into the atmosphere. Without it, the surface would overheat and the upper air would be far too cold. It is also the engine behind thunderstorms, which are essentially convection cells that have grown explosively large.

Convection cells form when heated air rises, cools, spreads, and sinks. Atmospheric stability — determined by the lapse rate — controls whether convection is gentle or explosive. Convection is the engine of weather.

Moisture & Clouds

The atmosphere carries water in its invisible form: vapor. Water evaporates from oceans, lakes, rivers, and soil, mixing into the air. The amount of vapor air can hold depends on temperature — warm air holds dramatically more than cold air. At 30 °C, a cubic meter of air can hold about 30 grams of water vapor. At 0 °C, only about 5 grams.

Relative humidity measures how close the air is to its capacity. At 100% relative humidity, the air is saturated — it's holding all the vapor it can. The temperature at which a given parcel of air reaches saturation is called the dew point.

This is where convection and moisture intersect. As air rises and cools adiabatically, its capacity to hold vapor drops. Eventually the temperature falls to the dew point, the air becomes saturated, and water vapor condenses into tiny droplets — forming a cloud. The altitude where this happens is the cloud base, and you can estimate it from the temperature and dew point at the surface.

A rising air parcel cools at the dry adiabatic rate until the temperature meets the dew point. That's where the cloud forms.

Condensation also releases latent heat. When water evaporated at the surface, it absorbed energy. Now, as it condenses back into liquid, it returns that energy to the air as heat. This is hugely important: latent heat release warms the rising air, making it more buoyant, which makes it rise faster, which causes more condensation, which releases more heat. It's a positive feedback loop — and it's what turns ordinary convection into towering cumulonimbus clouds and thunderstorms.

cloud base ≈ 125 × (Tsurface − Tdew point) meters

Above the cloud base, the rising air is now saturated, and it cools more slowly — at the moist adiabatic lapse rate (about 5–6 °C/km instead of 10), because the latent heat of condensation partially offsets the cooling from expansion. This is why the tallest clouds — cumulonimbus — can punch through the troposphere into the stratosphere: the moisture keeps feeding them energy.

Clouds form when rising air cools to the dew point. The condensation releases latent heat, which fuels further rising — a positive feedback loop that powers the most dramatic weather phenomena.

Terrain

So far, the forces that lift air — convection, fronts — come from the atmosphere itself. But the most direct way to force air upward is to put a mountain in its path. When wind encounters a mountain range, it has nowhere to go but up. This is orographic lift, and it connects straight back to what we learned about clouds: rising air cools, and cooled air condenses.

The windward side of a mountain range — the side facing the incoming wind — is where this lift is strongest. As moist air is pushed upward along the slope, it cools at the dry adiabatic rate until it hits the dew point and clouds form. Continued ascent wrings moisture out as precipitation. Some of the wettest places on earth owe their rainfall to orographic lift: the western Cascades, the southern Himalayas, the Scottish Highlands. Cherrapunji, India, in the path of monsoon winds hitting the Khasi Hills, receives over 11 meters of rain per year.

On the other side of the mountain — the leeward side — something different happens. The air, having lost much of its moisture as rain on the windward slopes, now descends. Descending air compresses and warms, but because it lost water on the way up, it warms at the dry adiabatic rate (10 °C/km) rather than the slower moist rate (5–6 °C/km) it cooled at during ascent. The result: the air arrives on the leeward side warmer and drier than it started. This is the rain shadow.

Wind flows left to right over the mountain. Clouds and rain form on the windward side where air is forced upward. The leeward side sits in the rain shadow — warm and dry.

These warm, dry lee winds have names all over the world: Foehn in the Alps, Chinook in the Rockies, Santa Ana in southern California. A Chinook can raise temperatures by 10–20 °C in a matter of hours, melting snow so fast that early settlers called it the "snow eater." Santa Ana winds, hot and bone-dry after descending from the interior mountains, are the driving force behind California's worst wildfire seasons.

Rain shadows operate at every scale. The Rockies create a continental rain shadow across the Great Plains. The Andes squeeze moisture from Pacific trade winds, leaving the Atacama Desert — the driest place on earth — in their lee. Even a modest hill can create a local dry pocket on its downwind side. Wherever terrain interrupts the flow of moist air, it reshapes precipitation patterns and, over time, entire ecosystems.

Mountains force air upward, producing clouds and precipitation on the windward side. The leeward side receives warm, dry descending air — the rain shadow. Terrain shapes precipitation patterns at every scale, from local hills to continental mountain ranges.

Air Masses & Fronts

An air mass is a large body of air — sometimes thousands of kilometers across — with roughly uniform temperature and humidity. Air masses form when air sits over a region long enough to take on its characteristics. Air over the tropical ocean becomes warm and moist. Air over the arctic becomes cold and dry.

Weather happens when different air masses collide. Because they have different densities (warm air is lighter, cold air is heavier), they don't mix easily. Instead, one slides over or under the other, creating a boundary called a front.

Cold fronts

When a cold air mass advances on a warm one, the dense cold air acts like a wedge, pushing under the warm air and forcing it rapidly upward. This violent lifting produces a narrow band of intense weather — tall cumulonimbus clouds, heavy rain or hail, sometimes severe thunderstorms. Cold fronts move fast and the weather they bring, while dramatic, passes quickly.

Warm fronts

When warm air advances on a cold mass, it can't shove the cold air out of the way — cold air is too dense. Instead, the warm air rides up and over the cold air along a gentle slope. This produces a wide area of gradual lifting, creating layered clouds (stratus, altostratus, cirrostratus) and prolonged, steady precipitation. Warm fronts move slowly and the associated weather can last for days.

Cross-section view of a front. Toggle between cold front (dense air wedging under) and warm front (warm air riding over).

Fronts are where much of the mid-latitude weather action happens. The boundary between a tropical and a polar air mass is where you find the strongest temperature gradients, the most vigorous lifting, and the stormiest conditions. This boundary zone — the polar front — meanders around the globe and is intimately connected to the jet stream.

Air masses are large regions of uniform temperature and humidity. When they collide, the boundary — a front — forces air upward and produces weather. Cold fronts are steep and violent; warm fronts are gentle and prolonged.

Storms

A thunderstorm is convection that has gone into overdrive. The recipe requires three ingredients: instability (a steep lapse rate so that rising air keeps accelerating), moisture (fuel for latent heat release), and lift (something to get the air moving upward in the first place — a front, a mountain, or strong surface heating).

In the developing stage, a powerful updraft dominates. Warm, moist air surges upward, sometimes at speeds exceeding 30 m/s. As it rises, moisture condenses and the cloud grows explosively — a cumulus tower can reach 12 km in altitude in 20 minutes. The condensation releases enormous amounts of latent heat, which drives the updraft even harder.

The mature stage begins when the accumulated water becomes too heavy for the updraft to support. Rain and hail begin falling, dragging air downward and creating a downdraft alongside the updraft. This stage produces the most intense weather: heavy rain, lightning, strong winds, and sometimes hail or tornadoes. The storm is now a heat engine, extracting energy from warm, moist air and converting it into kinetic energy and precipitation.

In the dissipating stage, the downdraft spreads along the surface and cuts off the inflow of warm air that was feeding the updraft. Starved of fuel, the storm weakens and decays, leaving behind a spread of high cirrus clouds — the anvil — as the only reminder.

A thunderstorm cell progresses through its life cycle. Updraft (red arrows), downdraft (blue arrows), and rain. Wind shear tilts the storm, separating updraft and downdraft to extend its life.

A crucial modifier is wind shear — the change in wind speed or direction with altitude. Without shear, the downdraft falls right on top of the updraft and kills the storm within 30 minutes. With shear, the storm tilts: the updraft and downdraft become separated, and the storm can sustain itself for hours. The most violent storms — supercells — exist in environments with strong shear and extreme instability. Their rotating updrafts can produce tornadoes.

Storms require instability, moisture, and lift. The updraft drives condensation and latent heat release; the downdraft brings rain and spreads cold air. Wind shear determines whether a storm dies quickly or organizes into a long-lived, severe system.

Global Circulation

Zoom out from individual storms to the whole planet, and a grand pattern emerges. The equator receives far more solar energy than the poles. This creates a massive temperature gradient from equator to pole, and the atmosphere's primary job is to transport heat poleward to reduce that imbalance.

If the earth didn't rotate, you'd get a single, simple convection cell in each hemisphere: air would rise at the equator, flow poleward at altitude, sink at the pole, and return along the surface. But the Coriolis effect breaks this single cell into three distinct cells per hemisphere.

The Hadley cell

Nearest the equator, intense heating drives air upward in a band of thunderstorms called the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ). This air flows poleward at altitude, but the Coriolis effect deflects it eastward. By about 30° latitude, it has been deflected so much that it piles up and sinks. This sinking air creates the subtropical highs — persistent high-pressure belts that produce the world's great deserts (Sahara, Arabian, Sonoran). The surface return flow, deflected westward by Coriolis, creates the trade winds.

The Ferrel cell

Between 30° and 60° latitude, the circulation is indirect — driven by the cells on either side rather than by direct thermal forcing. Surface winds in this belt are deflected eastward by Coriolis, creating the westerlies. This is why weather systems in the mid-latitudes generally move from west to east.

The Polar cell

Cold air sinks at the poles, flows toward mid-latitudes at the surface (deflected westward, creating the polar easterlies), rises at around 60° where it meets the warmer Ferrel cell air, and returns poleward aloft.

Cross-section from equator to pole showing the three circulation cells. Surface winds are labeled. Adjust rotation to see how the Coriolis effect shapes the cells.

At the boundaries between cells — particularly at around 60°, where polar and tropical air collide — the temperature gradient is steep and the jet stream forms. This ribbon of fast-moving air at about 10 km altitude (250+ km/h) steers weather systems and marks the polar front. When the jet stream dips southward, it pulls cold air with it; when it bulges northward, it allows warm air to push poleward. These meanders are the direct cause of the week-to-week weather variability in temperate regions.

Global circulation distributes heat from the equator to the poles through three cells per hemisphere: Hadley, Ferrel, and Polar. The Coriolis effect shapes these cells and creates the trade winds, westerlies, and jet streams that govern weather patterns.

Putting It All Together

Each concept we've explored — heating, pressure, wind, Coriolis deflection, convection, moisture, terrain, fronts, storms, and global circulation — is a piece of the puzzle. On their own, they explain isolated phenomena. Combined, they produce the full complexity of weather.

The chain of causation runs like this:

1. Differential heating — The sun heats the earth's surface unevenly. Land and water, equator and poles, day and night all create temperature contrasts.

2. Pressure gradients form — Temperature differences create density differences, which produce pressure differences at the surface and aloft.

3. Air moves — Wind flows from high pressure to low, shaped by the Coriolis effect into spiraling patterns around pressure centers.

4. Moisture enters the picture — As air flows over water and warm surfaces, it picks up moisture. When forced upward — by convection, fronts, or terrain — the air cools, vapor condenses, clouds form, and latent heat is released.

5. Storms develop — Where instability, moisture, and lifting converge, convection intensifies into storms. Latent heat release amplifies the process.

6. The global machine — All of this is organized by the planet's rotation into the great circulation cells, jet streams, and migrating pressure systems that define climate zones and steer daily weather.

What's remarkable is how few fundamental principles underlie all of this. The gas laws. Gravity. The conservation of energy and momentum. The rotation of the earth. From these simple rules, the full spectrum of weather emerges — from a gentle morning breeze to a category-5 hurricane.

Modern weather forecasting works by encoding these principles into systems of differential equations and solving them numerically on supercomputers, starting from measured initial conditions. The fact that we can predict the weather five to seven days out is a testament to how well we understand these underlying systems. And the fact that we can't reliably predict beyond two weeks is a testament to how exquisitely sensitive the atmosphere is to small perturbations — the deterministic chaos that Edward Lorenz discovered while studying these very equations.
