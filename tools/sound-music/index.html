What Is Sound?

Drop a pebble into still water and ripples spread outward. Sound works the same way, except the medium is air (or any material) and the ripples are invisible fluctuations in pressure.

When a guitar string vibrates, it pushes the air molecules next to it closer together — a region of compression. As the string swings back, it leaves behind a region of lower pressure — rarefaction. These alternating zones of high and low pressure propagate outward through the air as a longitudinal wave. The molecules themselves don't travel from the guitar to your ear; they just jostle back and forth, passing the pattern along like a crowd doing the wave in a stadium.

Drag the sound source to move it. Waves reflect off the walls, creating interference patterns.

The speed of this wave depends on the medium. In air at room temperature, sound travels at about 343 meters per second (roughly 767 mph). In water, it's about 1,480 m/s. In steel, over 5,000 m/s. The denser and stiffer the material, the faster the wave propagates.

In a vacuum, there's nothing to compress and nothing to carry the pattern. Sound requires a medium — which is why, despite what Hollywood suggests, explosions in space are silent.

Sound is a pattern of pressure traveling through a medium, not matter moving through space. It's a wave of information, not a wave of stuff.

Anatomy of a Wave

To talk precisely about sound, we need a vocabulary for describing waves. The simplest wave is a sine wave — a smooth, endlessly repeating oscillation. It's the building block from which all other sounds can be constructed.

A sine wave is described by a handful of properties:

Frequency is how many complete cycles the wave completes per second, measured in hertz (Hz). A wave that completes 440 cycles per second has a frequency of 440 Hz — this happens to be the note A above middle C, the standard tuning reference.

Amplitude is the height of the wave — how far the pressure swings above and below the resting level. Larger amplitude means louder sound.

Wavelength (λ) is the physical distance between two identical points on consecutive cycles — from peak to peak, or trough to trough. Wavelength and frequency are inversely related: as frequency goes up, wavelength shrinks.

Period is the time for one complete cycle — the inverse of frequency.

These relationships are fundamental. If you know the speed of sound in air (343 m/s) and the frequency of a note (say 440 Hz), you can calculate the wavelength: 343 / 440 ≈ 0.78 meters. Concert A has a wavelength of about 78 centimeters — roughly the length of your arm.

Phase describes where in its cycle a wave starts. Two waves with the same frequency and amplitude but different phases are offset in time. Phase matters enormously when waves interact — when two identical waves are perfectly in phase they reinforce each other, and when they're half a cycle out of phase they cancel.

Pitch and Perception

Frequency is a physical measurement. Pitch is the psychological experience — how high or low a sound seems to us. The two are closely related, but the mapping is not straightforward.

Humans can hear frequencies from roughly 20 Hz (a deep, rumbling bass) to about 20,000 Hz (a piercing, barely audible whine). This range spans about 10 octaves. Most music lives in the middle — the piano covers roughly 27.5 Hz (A0) to 4,186 Hz (C8).

The crucial insight is that our perception of pitch is logarithmic, not linear. Going from 100 Hz to 200 Hz sounds like the same "step up" as going from 1,000 Hz to 2,000 Hz — even though the second jump is ten times larger in absolute terms. What matters is the ratio. In both cases, the frequency doubled, and a doubling of frequency corresponds to one octave.

Click and drag left/right to change frequency. Notice how equal distances don't sound like equal pitch steps — perception is logarithmic.

This logarithmic hearing isn't arbitrary — it's deeply useful. It means we can perceive fine distinctions among the low frequencies that dominate in nature (animal calls, thunder, flowing water) while still being sensitive to the high frequencies that carry detail (consonants in speech, rustling leaves).

The standard tuning reference is concert A = 440 Hz, adopted internationally in 1955. All other note frequencies in the modern system are defined relative to this anchor point.

Our perception of pitch is logarithmic. Each octave is a doubling of frequency, yet sounds like an equal step up. This single fact shapes the entire structure of music.

Waveforms

A sine wave is the purest sound — a single frequency with no complexity. But almost nothing in the real world produces a pure sine. When you pluck a string, blow into a flute, or speak a vowel, the sound wave has a distinctive shape that's far more complex. The shape of the wave — its waveform — determines its sonic character.

Four classic waveforms appear constantly in synthesis and audio:

Sine — smooth and pure. Contains only the fundamental frequency. Sounds hollow, clean, almost electronic. This is the "atom" of sound.

Square — alternates abruptly between two levels. Sounds hollow and buzzy, like a vintage video game. Contains only odd harmonics (3rd, 5th, 7th...) with amplitudes that fall off as 1/n.

Triangle — a softer, rounded version of the square wave. Also contains only odd harmonics, but they fall off much faster (as 1/n²), giving it a mellower tone.

Sawtooth — ramps up linearly then drops sharply. The richest of the four — it contains all harmonics, both odd and even, with amplitudes falling off as 1/n. Sounds bright and buzzy. This is the starting point for many synthesizer patches.

The four fundamental waveforms. Each has a distinctive visual shape and a distinctive sound, determined by its harmonic content.

The key insight here is one of the most beautiful results in mathematics: any periodic waveform, no matter how complex, can be decomposed into a sum of sine waves at different frequencies and amplitudes. This is Fourier's theorem, and it means that the difference between a square wave and a sine wave isn't a difference in kind — it's a difference in recipe.

Harmonics and Timbre

When a violin and a trumpet both play the note A4 (440 Hz), you hear the same pitch — but the sounds are unmistakably different. The property that distinguishes them is timbre (pronounced "TAM-ber"), and it arises from the harmonic structure of each instrument's sound.

When a string vibrates, it doesn't just vibrate as a whole — it simultaneously vibrates in halves, thirds, quarters, and so on. Each mode of vibration produces a frequency that is an integer multiple of the fundamental. These are the harmonics (also called overtones or partials).

If the fundamental is 440 Hz, the harmonic series is:

440, 880, 1320, 1760, 2200, 2640, 3080 ... (f, 2f, 3f, 4f, 5f, 6f, 7f ...)

Every instrument produces these same frequencies when playing the same note. What differs is the relative strength of each harmonic. A flute produces a nearly pure fundamental with weak upper harmonics. A trumpet has strong upper harmonics that give it brightness and bite. An oboe has an unusually strong set of odd harmonics, giving it that nasal, penetrating quality.

This brings us to one of the most profound ideas in acoustics: Fourier's theorem. The French mathematician Joseph Fourier proved in 1822 that any periodic function — any repeating wave, no matter how jagged or complex — can be expressed as a sum of sine waves at specific frequencies and amplitudes.

This means sound has a dual nature. You can describe it as a waveform in time (the shape of the pressure fluctuation) or as a spectrum in frequency (the recipe of sine wave components). Both descriptions are complete and equivalent — they're just two ways of looking at the same thing. The mathematical operation that converts between them is called the Fourier transform.

Every sound can be decomposed into sine waves at different frequencies and amplitudes. The recipe of harmonics is what gives each instrument its voice — its timbre.

The Octave

Of all the possible frequency relationships between two notes, one stands apart as unique: the octave, a ratio of 2:1.

Play a note at 440 Hz, then play one at 880 Hz. Something remarkable happens — the higher note sounds like "the same note, but higher." This phenomenon is called octave equivalence, and it's one of the very few aspects of music perception that appears to be universal across all human cultures. Every musical tradition in the world, from Javanese gamelan to European classical to West African drumming, treats notes an octave apart as fundamentally equivalent.

The reason is physical. When two notes are an octave apart, every harmonic of the higher note is also a harmonic of the lower note. A note at 880 Hz has harmonics at 1760, 2640, 3520... — which are exactly the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th harmonics of 440 Hz. The two sounds share so much harmonic content that our auditory system perceives them as essentially the same pitch class.

The lower wave completes one cycle for every two cycles of the upper wave. Despite the frequency difference, they sound like the same note.

Because of octave equivalence, music is organized in a fundamentally circular way. We don't need an infinite number of note names — we just need enough to fill one octave, and then the pattern repeats. The question, then, becomes: how do we divide the octave? How many notes should there be between one A and the next?

Consonance and Simple Ratios

Not all combinations of notes sound equally pleasant. Some pairs blend together smoothly — these are consonant intervals. Others create tension and roughness — these are dissonant. The ancient Greeks discovered that consonance corresponds to simple frequency ratios.

The story goes that Pythagoras, walking past a blacksmith's shop, noticed that certain pairs of hammers produced pleasant sounds when struck together. He investigated and found that the pleasing pairs had weights in simple whole-number ratios. Whether the story is true or not, the underlying principle is real and foundational.

The most consonant interval after the octave (2:1) is the perfect fifth, with a ratio of 3:2. If your base note is 440 Hz, the perfect fifth above it is 660 Hz. This interval sounds open, stable, and powerful — it's the interval between the first two different notes of "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star."

Next comes the perfect fourth at 4:3 (about 587 Hz above our 440). Then the major third at 5:4 (550 Hz) — the interval that gives major chords their bright, happy quality.

Why do simple ratios sound consonant? When two notes have harmonics that overlap, the combined sound wave is smooth and periodic. When they don't, the harmonics fall close to each other but not quite on top of each other, creating rapid amplitude fluctuations called beating. When the beating is fast enough — roughly 20 to 50 times per second — we perceive it as roughness, the sensation that makes dissonance feel uncomfortable.

Sweep through different frequency ratios. Simple ratios (3:2, 4:3, 5:4) produce smooth, stable waveforms. Complex ratios create beating and roughness.

Intervals that sound "good" together correspond to simple frequency ratios. When harmonics align, the combined wave is stable and periodic. When they don't, you hear beating and roughness.

Why Twelve Notes?

This is the central question of Western music theory: why do we divide the octave into exactly twelve notes? The answer involves one of the most elegant compromises in the history of mathematics.

Building a scale from fifths

Since the perfect fifth (3:2) is the most consonant interval after the octave, a natural approach is to build a musical scale by stacking fifths. Start on a note — say C. Go up a fifth to G. Up another fifth to D. Continue: A, E, B, F#, C#, G#, D#, A#, F... and after twelve fifths, you arrive at a note that should be the same C you started on, seven octaves higher.

But it isn't. Twelve perfect fifths gives you a frequency ratio of (3/2)12 = 129.746..., while seven octaves gives you 27 = 128. The gap between these two values is called the Pythagorean comma — about 23.46 cents (roughly a quarter of a semitone). The circle of fifths doesn't quite close.

Stacking perfect fifths creates a spiral, not a circle. The gap at the end — the Pythagorean comma — is the fundamental problem of tuning.

The tuning problem

This mismatch is a mathematical fact that no amount of cleverness can eliminate. You cannot have perfectly pure fifths and perfectly closed octaves. Something has to give.

Throughout history, musicians tried different compromises. Pythagorean tuning kept the fifths pure and accepted that some intervals would be badly out of tune. Just intonation tuned each interval to its simplest ratio (3:2 for fifths, 5:4 for major thirds), but this meant that different keys had different characters — some beautiful, some unusable. Meantone temperament slightly narrowed the fifths to make the thirds purer, but this still left some keys sounding awful (the notorious "wolf fifth").

Equal temperament: the great compromise

The solution that eventually won out — and that defines virtually all Western music today — is equal temperament. The idea is radically simple: divide the octave into twelve exactly equal steps. Each step (a semitone) is the same frequency ratio:

semitone ratio = 21/12 ≈ 1.05946

To find the frequency of any note, you just count how many semitones it is from a reference pitch and multiply:

f(n) = f0 × 2n/12

where f0 is the reference frequency (440 Hz for A4) and n is the number of semitones above or below it.

The trade-off is that no interval except the octave is perfectly pure. The equal-tempered fifth is 27/12 ≈ 1.4983, not 1.5 (the pure 3:2). The equal-tempered major third is 24/12 ≈ 1.2599, not 1.25 (the pure 5:4). These differences are small — about 2 cents for the fifth, 14 cents for the third — and most listeners can't distinguish them.

Compare an interval in just intonation (pure ratio) vs equal temperament. The difference is subtle but real.

What equal temperament sacrifices in purity, it gains in freedom. Because every key uses exactly the same interval structure, you can play in any key and modulate freely between keys without anything sounding "wrong." This symmetry was revolutionary — it enabled the chromatic harmony of Bach, the key changes of Beethoven, the jazz of Coltrane, and essentially all of modern popular music.

Bach's The Well-Tempered Clavier (1722) was a landmark work specifically designed to demonstrate this freedom — 24 preludes and fugues, one in every major and minor key, all playable on a single instrument tuned in a (nearly) equal temperament.

The 12-tone system is a mathematical compromise. We sacrifice the purity of individual intervals to gain the ability to play in any key. This single trade-off enabled centuries of Western harmonic music.

Scales and the Keyboard

Equal temperament gives us twelve equally spaced notes per octave. This complete set is the chromatic scale: C, C#, D, D#, E, F, F#, G, G#, A, A#, B — and then the pattern repeats.

But twelve notes at once is a lot. Most music uses subsets — seven-note scales that select certain intervals and skip others, creating a characteristic pattern of whole steps and half steps.

The major scale follows the pattern: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half (W-W-H-W-W-W-H). Starting on C, this gives you C-D-E-F-G-A-B — all the white keys on a piano. The major scale sounds bright and resolved; it's the "do-re-mi" of everyday music.

The natural minor scale uses a different pattern: W-H-W-W-H-W-W. Starting on A, you get A-B-C-D-E-F-G — again, all white keys, but the different starting point changes the pattern of intervals, giving the minor scale its darker, more melancholic character.

The piano keyboard

The piano keyboard is a physical map of the chromatic scale. White keys are the notes of the C major scale. Black keys fill in the remaining five chromatic notes (the sharps/flats). This is why the black keys appear in groups of two and three — and why there are no black keys between E and F, or between B and C. Those are the places where the major scale already has a half step.

It's worth noting that the major and minor scales are just two of many possible seven-note selections from the twelve chromatic notes. The modes — Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, and others — are alternative selections with their own distinctive moods. And beyond Western music, cultures around the world have developed scales with different numbers of notes: the pentatonic scale (five notes, found in blues, rock, and many folk traditions), the whole-tone scale (six notes), and microtonal systems with more than twelve notes per octave.

Seeing and Building Sound

Throughout this article, we've described sound in two ways: as a waveform (pressure over time) and as a spectrum (energy at each frequency). There's a powerful visualization that combines both: the spectrogram.

A spectrogram plots frequency on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis, using color or brightness to represent amplitude. It's a complete picture of a sound: you can see the fundamental and harmonics as horizontal bands, watch them shift as pitch changes, and observe how the harmonic recipe evolves over the life of a note.

Learning to read a spectrogram is like learning to read a language. A pure sine wave appears as a single horizontal line. A sawtooth wave appears as a stack of evenly-spaced lines (its harmonics). A chord appears as multiple groups of harmonic stacks. Speech appears as constantly shifting ribbons of energy — the formants that distinguish vowels from each other.

Synthesis: building sound from scratch

If every sound can be decomposed into sine waves, then every sound can also be built from sine waves. This is additive synthesis: start with silence and add sine wave components one by one, each at a specific frequency, amplitude, and phase, until you've reconstructed the target sound.

In practice, additive synthesis can require dozens or hundreds of individually controlled sine waves to sound convincing. So synthesizers often use the opposite approach: subtractive synthesis. Start with a harmonically rich waveform (like a sawtooth) and use filters to carve away unwanted frequencies, sculpting the timbre like a sculptor removing marble to reveal a form.

A third approach is FM (frequency modulation) synthesis, where one wave modulates the frequency of another. This can produce extraordinarily complex and evolving timbres from just two oscillators — it's the technology behind the iconic Yamaha DX7, one of the best-selling synthesizers of all time.

Digital audio

All of these ideas converge in digital audio. A microphone converts pressure waves into an electrical signal. An analog-to-digital converter samples that signal thousands of times per second — typically 44,100 times (CD quality) or 48,000 times (professional audio). Each sample is a number representing the pressure at that instant.

The Nyquist theorem guarantees that if you sample at twice the highest frequency present in the signal, you can perfectly reconstruct the original continuous waveform. Since human hearing tops out at about 20 kHz, a 44.1 kHz sample rate captures everything we can hear.

From the vibrations of a guitar string to the mathematics of Fourier transforms, from the Pythagorean comma to the precision of digital sampling — sound and music sit at a remarkable intersection of physics, mathematics, perception, and culture. Every note you hear is a pressure wave. Every melody is a pattern of frequency ratios. And the twelve-tone system that underlies most of the music you know is a centuries-old compromise between mathematical purity and practical freedom.

Every sound can be both built from scratch (synthesis) and taken apart into components (analysis). The spectrogram is the bridge — a way to see what we hear.
