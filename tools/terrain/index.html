The Problem with Randomness

If you've ever played a game with an infinite world, or watched a film with sweeping shots over computer-generated mountains, you've seen procedural terrain. The landscapes aren't hand-sculpted — they're grown from algorithms. But how do you get from code to something that looks like nature?

The most naive approach is the most tempting: just pick a random height for each point. After all, terrain is irregular, and randomness is about as irregular as you can get.

Each bar is an independently random height. Drag to regenerate.

The result is hopeless. Every point is completely unrelated to its neighbors, so you get a violent, spiky mess that looks nothing like real terrain. Nature doesn't work this way — the height of a hillside at one point is closely related to the height a meter away. There is spatial correlation.

Real terrain is locally smooth but globally varied. We need noise that respects this: nearby points should have similar values, while distant points can differ freely.

To get there, we need to replace pure randomness with something more structured. We need smooth noise.

Making Noise Smooth

The idea behind smooth noise is simple. Instead of assigning a random value to every single point, we only pick random values at regular intervals — let's call these lattice points. For everything in between, we smoothly blend from one lattice value to the next.

This blending is called interpolation, and the choice of interpolation method matters more than you might think. The simplest option is linear interpolation: draw a straight line between each pair of lattice points. It's fast, but it creates sharp kinks at every lattice point, and those kinks are clearly visible.

Dots mark the random lattice points. The curve interpolates between them. Adjust frequency to change how many lattice points fit in the space.

A much better choice is a smooth-step function — one that starts and ends with zero slope, producing a curve that flows gently through each lattice point. Ken Perlin used the polynomial 6t5 - 15t4 + 10t3, which has the nice property that both its first and second derivatives are zero at the lattice points. This means no visible kinks and no sudden changes in curvature.

Frequency and wavelength

Notice the frequency control above. When frequency is low, the lattice points are far apart and the noise has broad, gentle hills. Crank it up and you get tight, rapid variation. The frequency controls the scale of detail — and this turns out to be the single most important lever in procedural terrain.

Frequency determines the scale of features in noise. Low frequency produces large, rolling hills. High frequency produces fine detail. Real terrain has features at every scale simultaneously.

But a single frequency of noise still looks too simple and regular to pass for terrain. Real mountains have broad slopes and small bumps and tiny rocks — detail at every scale. To get there, we need to combine noise at multiple frequencies.

Layering Detail

The technique is beautifully simple: take your smooth noise function and call it multiple times, each time doubling the frequency and halving the amplitude. Then add up all the results.

The first layer — the lowest frequency — defines the broad shape: the mountains and valleys. The second layer adds medium-scale variation: ridges and foothills. Each successive layer contributes finer and finer detail. These layers are called octaves, borrowing the musical term because each one doubles in frequency, just like notes an octave apart.

Each colored line is one octave. The white line is their sum. Persistence controls how much each octave contributes. Lacunarity controls the frequency multiplier between octaves.

Two parameters control the layering:

Persistence (sometimes called gain) determines how much each successive octave contributes relative to the last. A persistence of 0.5 means each octave has half the amplitude of the one before it. Lower values produce smoother terrain; higher values make the fine detail more prominent.

Lacunarity controls the frequency ratio between octaves. The standard value is 2.0 (each octave is double the frequency), but you can adjust it for different characteristics.

result = ∑ amplitudei × noise(position × frequencyi)

This sum-of-octaves approach is formally known as fractal Brownian motion (fBm). The name reflects the fact that natural terrain is fractal — it has statistical self-similarity at different scales. A coastline looks equally jagged whether you view it from an airplane or from the ground. Our layered noise captures exactly this property.

Fractal Brownian motion (fBm) layers multiple octaves of noise to create detail at every scale. This is the fundamental building block of procedural terrain.

Gradient Noise

The noise we've built so far — assigning random values at lattice points and interpolating — is called value noise. It works, but it has a subtle problem. Because the random values are independently chosen, the noise tends to develop a blocky, grid-aligned character, especially when you look at it in two dimensions. The lattice structure shows through.

In 1983, Ken Perlin had a key insight. Instead of assigning random values at each lattice point, assign random gradients — directions. The noise value at any point is determined by how far it is from each surrounding lattice point, dotted with that point's gradient. This small change has a profound effect: the noise becomes zero at every lattice point, and the features arise from the interplay of gradients, producing a much more organic result.

Value noise (left) vs gradient noise (right). Notice how the gradient version lacks the grid-aligned artifacts and has a more natural flow.

The gradient approach also composes better with fBm — the octaves blend without reinforcing the grid pattern. This is why Perlin noise became the standard for procedural generation and won Ken Perlin an Academy Award for Technical Achievement.

In 2001, Perlin improved his algorithm further with simplex noise, which uses a triangular (simplex) grid instead of a square one and scales better to higher dimensions. More recently, open-source alternatives like OpenSimplex have become popular. For our purposes, the exact variant matters less than the core principle: gradients produce better noise than values.

Going to Two Dimensions

Everything we've done so far has been in one dimension — a single line of heights. To make terrain, we need a two-dimensional noise function: give it an (x, y) coordinate, get back a height value.

The extension is natural. Instead of lattice points on a line, we have a grid of lattice points on a plane. Each lattice point gets a random 2D gradient vector. To evaluate the noise at any point, we look at the four surrounding lattice points (or six, for simplex noise), compute the dot product of each gradient with the displacement vector to our point, and interpolate the results.

The result can be visualized as a grayscale image: a heightmap. Each pixel's brightness represents height — black for the lowest points, white for the highest.

A 2D heightmap generated with fractal noise. Adjust parameters to see how they affect the landscape structure.

Already you can start to see terrain-like structure. The broad light regions are highlands; the dark areas are valleys. But it's still just a flat image. To really feel like terrain, we need to lift it into three dimensions.

From Heightmap to Terrain

A heightmap is a grid of numbers. To turn it into a visible landscape, we create a mesh of triangles — a 3D surface where each vertex's vertical position is set by the corresponding heightmap value.

The process is straightforward: lay out a flat grid of vertices, then push each one up or down according to the noise value at its position. Connect adjacent vertices into triangles, compute surface normals for lighting, and you have a terrain mesh.

A 3D terrain mesh. Click and drag to orbit. Scroll to zoom. Toggle wireframe to see the triangle structure.

Even with basic flat shading, the shape of the land is immediately recognizable. But it still doesn't quite look right. The noise produces a statistically uniform surface — every region has roughly the same character. Real terrain isn't like that. Valleys are flat, ridges are sharp, and coastlines have a particular shape. We need to sculpt the raw noise.

Shaping the Land

Raw noise gives us a reasonable starting distribution of heights, but real terrain has specific structural properties that noise alone can't capture. We can fix this by applying mathematical operations to the noise output — think of them as sculpting tools.

Redistribution

The simplest transformation is to pass the noise through a power function. Raising the height to an exponent greater than 1 pushes low values lower while keeping high values high, creating flat plains with sharply rising peaks. An exponent less than 1 does the opposite — flattening the peaks while lifting the valleys.

height' = heightexponent

Terracing

Another useful technique is terracing: quantizing the height values into discrete steps. This creates plateau-like formations with sharp cliff edges between them. By blending between the original and terraced values, you can control how pronounced the effect is.

Domain warping

Perhaps the most powerful shaping technique is domain warping. Instead of transforming the output of the noise function, we transform its input. Before evaluating noise at a point, we offset that point by the value of another noise function. This warps the coordinate space, bending and stretching the noise in organic, swirling patterns.

height = noise(position + noise(position))
Experiment with different shaping parameters. The base noise is the same — only the post-processing changes.

Domain warping is particularly interesting because it can create shapes that look almost geological — winding river valleys, natural-looking mountain chains, and irregular coastlines. It's used extensively in film VFX to add visual complexity to terrain.

Shaping functions transform raw noise into terrain with specific character. Redistribution controls the height profile, terracing creates cliffs and plateaus, and domain warping adds organic distortion.

Adding Color

A gray terrain mesh is structurally correct but visually lifeless. To make it convincing, we map height to color, mimicking how real biomes distribute across elevation.

The idea is a color ramp — a function that takes a height value (0 to 1) and returns a color. Deep areas are dark blue (water). Just above the waterline is sandy beach. Moving upward: grassland, forest, bare rock, and finally snow-capped peaks.

To avoid perfectly horizontal color bands, we can introduce some noise into the thresholds, or blend between biomes over a transition zone rather than switching abruptly. The slope of the terrain also matters — steep areas should show exposed rock regardless of elevation.

Height-based coloring gives the terrain biome zones. Adjust the water level and snow line to reshape the landscape.

The color ramp makes a dramatic difference. What was an abstract shape suddenly reads as a landscape — you can see where the water would collect, where forests would grow, and where the peaks break above the treeline.

Erosion

The terrain we've built so far is a good approximation, but it has a quality that's hard to pin down: it looks generated. Real landscapes don't just emerge from mathematical functions — they're shaped over millions of years by physical processes. The most important of these is erosion.

Hydraulic erosion

Water is the sculptor of the earth. Rain falls, collects into streams, and flows downhill. As it flows, it picks up sediment from the ground beneath it. Where the water slows down — in flat areas or wide basins — it deposits that sediment. Over time, this carves valleys, creates river channels, and smooths slopes into the characteristic shapes we recognize as natural.

To simulate this, we drop virtual water droplets onto the terrain. Each droplet follows the path of steepest descent, picking up sediment as it moves and depositing it when it slows down or pools. After thousands of droplets, the terrain develops the telltale signatures of water erosion: branching river networks, alluvial fans, and carved gorges.

Thermal erosion

Gravity also plays a role. When the slope between two adjacent points exceeds a threshold (the talus angle), material slides downhill. This is thermal erosion — the physical process that causes scree slopes at the base of cliffs and prevents impossibly steep formations from persisting.

Drag the slider to increase the number of water droplets. Watch how the terrain gradually develops natural drainage patterns.

Erosion is what separates "noise terrain" from "believable terrain." Even a simplified hydraulic erosion simulation dramatically improves the realism of procedurally generated landscapes.

The difference between pre- and post-erosion terrain is striking. Before erosion, the terrain has that smooth, blobby quality of raw noise. After, it's full of sharp ridges, V-shaped valleys, and the kind of fine-grained detail that our eyes associate with real rock and soil.

Putting It All Together

Each technique we've explored — smooth noise, fractal layering, gradient noise, shaping, coloring, and erosion — solves a specific problem. On their own, they're interesting. Combined, they produce something remarkable.

The pipeline is:

1. Generate base noise — Use gradient noise with fBm (4-8 octaves) to create the fundamental height distribution.

2. Shape the terrain — Apply redistribution to set the height profile, domain warping for organic distortion, and any special effects like terracing.

3. Simulate erosion — Run hydraulic and thermal erosion passes to carve realistic drainage patterns and smooth impossibly steep slopes.

4. Color by biome — Map the final heights (and slopes) to terrain colors, giving the landscape its visual identity.

The complete terrain pipeline. Change the seed for a new landscape. Orbit with mouse, scroll to zoom.

What's remarkable about this approach is its economy. The entire landscape — every ridge, valley, and plateau — is defined by a handful of parameters and a seed value. Change the seed and you get an entirely different world, equally detailed and equally plausible. This is the power of procedural generation: infinite variety from finite rules.

The techniques we've covered here are the foundation. Real-world terrain engines build on them with additional layers: vegetation placement using Poisson disk sampling, atmospheric scattering for distant mountains, level-of-detail systems for rendering terrain that stretches to the horizon. But at their core, they all start where we started — with a question about how to turn randomness into something that looks like nature.
